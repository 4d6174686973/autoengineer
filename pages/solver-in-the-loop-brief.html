Solver-in-the-Loop Design Search

Semi-automated exploration of engineering design spaces, judged by the customer's own solver, with the engineer kept in the loop at the gates that matter.

A closed loop that proposes candidate geometries, runs them through a validated simulator, discards anything unbuildable, and returns a design package with the evidence behind it. Aimed at problems currently explored by hand, a few dozen candidates at a time.

The bet

Engineers are not short of judgement. They are short of throughput. A design study explores tens of candidates, because each one costs a person a day of setup, meshing, debugging and interpretation. We run the same study ten thousand times, in their solver, against their metric, inside their constraints.

We never claim to be the better engineer. That argument cannot be won in a meeting and should not be attempted. We claim more coverage of the design space, and we let their own solver decide who found the better part. The customer keeps the physics, the sign-off and the credit.

01

Division of labour

Choosing the next design point is not where the time goes. In a real design study the numerical search is a small slice of the effort; the rest is deciding what to vary, building the model, keeping the solver from failing, and judging whether a result means anything.

That surrounding work is manual today, and it is what caps a study at a few dozen evaluations. It is also the part nobody has automated, which is where the opening is.

So the split is explicit and stated up front. Classical numerics does the searching. The agent does everything around it. Bayesian optimisation, evolutionary methods and adjoint gradients are used wherever they are the right tool, and we say so rather than dressing them up.

Where an agent earns its place

  • Formulation. Turning a loose goal into a parameterisation, objective and constraint set. This choice decides the answer more than the optimiser does, and no optimiser can make it.
  • Restructuring the space. Bayesian optimisation cannot add a design variable. An agent can propose a different topology class, an extra fin row, a different coil count, then hand the new space back to the optimiser.
  • Keeping the loop alive. Meshing failures, divergence, bad boundary conditions. A grid search dies here. This is the largest time sink in practice.
  • Sanity. Spotting that a result is mesh-dependent, unconverged, or outside the model assumptions.
  • Portability. Reading a new solver interface and writing the adapter. This is why we can move between problems instead of being one-trick.
  • Search over structure. Program and topology space, where FunSearch and AlphaEvolve showed real gains over hand design.

Where it does not

  • Continuous, low-dimensional, smooth problems. Bayesian optimisation and gradient methods win. Published results on language models as numerical optimisers are mixed at best.
  • Anything a grid already solves. If a clean grid over a well-defined space answers the question, the problem was already formulated, and formulation was the hard part. Those are not our customers.
  • Reinforcement learning. RL pays off when the same problem is solved thousands of times. Every customer geometry differs, so training cost does not amortise. Only worth it inside one narrow, repeated problem class.
  • Rewriting a validated solver core. Out of scope by design. See below.

Where the agent touches the solver itself

There is a narrow band where the agent improves the solve rather than just calling it: writing custom boundary conditions and user functions, selecting preconditioners and mesh adaptation strategies, generating problem-specific reduced-order models. Outside that band, and specifically for the validated core, it does not touch anything. Nobody would sign a drawing produced by a finite element code written last week.

The highest-value version is quieter than rewriting anything. Train a fast surrogate on the customer's own solver runs. That is a solver improvement in effect: it turns a twenty-minute solve into a millisecond one, which is what unlocks the ten thousand evaluations. It also produces an asset a competitor cannot copy, because it is fitted to their geometry family and their settings.

Where the human sits

The loop is built to run with a person in it, and to keep running as that person steps back. Autonomy is a setting on each gate, not a property of the system. Nothing here is designed to remove the engineer; it is designed to stop spending them on work that does not need judgement.

A customer starts at the top and moves down as evidence accumulates. Each level has an entry condition, so advancing is earned rather than argued.

L3 · Exception-only

Engineer sees only what is flagged

The checker decides what deserves attention. Everything clean passes through. This is where the throughput claim actually lands.

To advanceThree studies with no defect escaping to the customer
L4 · Unattended

Spec in, package out

The loop runs end to end against a signed spec. Note what has not changed: a person wrote the spec and a person signs the drawing. There is no level where nobody is accountable.

CeilingNot a goal for every customer. Many will sit at L2 forever and be happy.

The interaction budget

Human attention is the scarce resource in this system, not compute. Nobody can review ten thousand designs. Reviewing the parameterisation once takes twenty minutes and changes all ten thousand.

So the design rule is to spend review where leverage per minute is highest: heavy at stage A and B, near zero at D, selective at E. Escalations are batched, ranked by consequence, and rare by default. An interface that interrupts an engineer forty times a day has destroyed the thing it was built to deliver.

Tracked: interventions per validated design. It should fall run over run. If it is flat, the system is not learning anything from the person using it.

Judgement goes back in, not just out

An override that only fixes the design in front of it is wasted. Every correction is captured as a rule in the constraint library, so it is enforced on the next ten thousand candidates rather than caught by eye on one.

An engineer saying that fin spacing will not braze is worth more than the design they rejected. That sentence becomes a filter. Over a few engagements the library accumulates the manufacturing knowledge that is currently held in people's heads and nowhere else.

This is also the promotion mechanism. A gate earns automation when captured judgement makes it reliable, which is a measurable event rather than a decision someone has to feel comfortable with.

Two ways the human loop fails

  • Rubber stamping. The engineer approves everything without reading it, and review becomes theatre while everyone believes there is oversight. Watch the override rate: if it sits near zero, either the system is genuinely good or nobody is looking, and those two need to be told apart deliberately.
  • Bottlenecking. The person becomes the rate limiter and the throughput gain evaporates. A loop that evaluates ten thousand designs but needs a human decision every fifty has delivered nothing. Watch queue time per gate.

Six criteria, scored 1 to 5. The point is not that the numbers are true. The point is that disagreement becomes a disagreement about a specific score or weight, which can be settled.

score = 100 × Σ ( criterion × weight ) / Σ ( 5 × weight )

Method, and what is wrong with it

  • Weights encode strategy, not truth. Defaults are 3 / 3 / 2 / 2 / 3 / 2. Oracle, Loop and Proof carry the most weight because they govern whether we can show anything at all. Change them in the matrix and watch the order move. That is the intended use.
  • The scores are judgement, not measurement. Each of us should re-score the top ten independently. Where two of us differ by two points, that row needs a phone call with someone in the field, not another discussion.
  • The criteria are not independent. Loop and Proof correlate strongly. A problem cheap to verify is usually easy to prove. Treat them as one axis with two readings rather than two votes.
  • Three things are missing on purpose. Market size, competitive density, and how much we want to work on it. All three are real. None fit a 1 to 5 scale honestly, so they belong in the discussion instead of the sum.
  • High value plus low loop is the classic trap. Hypersonics, breeding blankets and new alloys all score 5 on value and 1 on closing the loop. They read as exciting and cannot be proven.

MVP structure

One problem, one customer geometry, one metric. The architecture is the same whichever candidate we pick, which is why we can afford to be wrong once.

Stages C and E are the moat. Anyone can wire a model to an optimiser. Encoding what a supplier can actually make, and knowing when a solve is lying to you, is the part that takes a year to copy.

Build order

Weeks 1–3

Benchmark first

Collect five real published or partner designs with known performance. These become the scoreboard. Nothing else gets built until we can measure against them.

OutputBenchmark suite with human baselines
Weeks 3–6

Dumb loop, end to end

Script the whole pipeline with no intelligence in it. Random or Latin hypercube sampling only. The goal is a headless solve that runs unattended overnight without dying.

OutputOne command, valid results by morning
Weeks 6–10

Surrogate, then search

Train a surrogate on the overnight runs, then run Bayesian or gradient-based optimisation on top. Add the agent layer only where it measurably beats the plain optimiser.

OutputBeat the benchmark on three of five cases
Weeks 10–13

Constraint library

Encode the manufacturing rules a real supplier applies. Re-run the benchmark with constraints on and record how much of the win survives. That number is the honest one.

OutputDesigns a shop will quote without redrawing
Weeks 12–16

Deliverable and demo

Auto-generated design report with side-by-side comparison. Plus one physical or third-party validated result if the part can be made in time.

OutputSomething we can put in front of a buyer

One number we track from week one

Validated designs per GPU-hour. Not model quality, not agent autonomy. Every architectural choice — surrogate accuracy, mesh coarsening, early stopping, cheap screening before expensive solves — is judged by whether it moves that number. It is also the number that transfers if we ever change domain.

07

Funding plan

Non-dilutive money first, because it is priced in paperwork rather than equity, and a paid pilot is the strongest signal we can show an investor.

Track A
Public grants

EXIST Gründerstipendium and NRW state programmes for the build period. EIC Accelerator and Eurostars for the round after. Deep-tech hardware fits these schemes better than most software.

Track B
Paid pilots

Two design studies at a fixed fee each. Revenue is small; the value is a reference customer, a real constraint set, and proof someone pays.

Track C
Pre-seed equity

Deep-tech angels and seed funds once the benchmark result exists. Approach with a measured win, not a deck. HTGF and comparable European deep-tech funds are the natural first calls.

Track D
Institutional access

A research collaboration with a lab or institute buys compute, credibility and solver expertise for the price of a joint publication.

Sequence

  • Apply while building. Applications need the benchmark plan, not the benchmark result.
  • Land the first pilot before the round. One signed study changes every investor conversation.
  • Raise on a measured number. Beating their design by a stated margin, in their solver, is the only slide that matters.
  • Keep the raise small. We need compute and solver licences, not headcount.
  • Check the licence bill early. Per-core commercial solver pricing can make large sweeps uneconomic. Favour open-source solvers, or run on the customer's own licences.

Go to market

Sell the outcome before the software. Productise only after we have won the same comparison three times.

Stage 1

Design as a service

We run the loop ourselves and deliver a validated design package at a fixed fee. Slow to scale, but it pays, and it teaches us which constraints actually bind.

Signal to watchDo they ask for a second study?
Stage 2

The bake-off

Standard entry motion. The customer names a part they are proud of and a metric. We get two weeks. Beating a design an engineer defends in person is worth more than any demo.

Signal to watchWin rate across three attempts
Stage 3

Deployed tool

Same pipeline, run by their engineers on their infrastructure, priced per seat with a compute component. Only after service work has hardened the constraint library.

Signal to watchRuns per week without us in the room

How we reach people

  • Publish the benchmark. An open comparison set with our results on it is the cheapest credibility available, and it makes competitors argue on our terms.
  • Go where the engineers are. Domain conferences and technical workshops, not startup events. The buyer is a principal engineer, and they read papers.
  • Start with the desperate. Teams already missing a thermal or schedule target will run a pilot in weeks. Comfortable teams take a year.
  • Partner with the fabricator. The shop that makes the part wants better designs to quote, and already has the customer list.
  • Sell the trail, not just the answer. Engineers reject black boxes because they have to defend the design. Ship the reasoning, the rejected alternatives and the convergence evidence alongside the geometry.
09

Risks and potential

What kills this, and what it becomes if it works.

What kills it

The trust gap

An engineer has to sign the drawing. If they cannot explain why the geometry looks the way it does, they will not sign, and a 20% win becomes a slide nobody acts on.

The simulation-to-reality gap

A 15% gain in CFD can be 3% on a rig. If our wins live only inside the model, we are selling optimism. Physical validation belongs in the first pilot, not the third.

Incumbents already ship optimisation

The large simulation vendors have optimisation modules, and several well-funded companies sell surrogate-based design today. We need a wedge they structurally cannot copy, which is the manufacturing constraint library, not the model.

Solver economics

Per-core commercial licences make large sweeps expensive. If the search costs more than the engineer it replaces, there is no business.

Access to real problems

The most valuable problems sit behind export control, NDAs or a year of procurement. We can be right about the technology and never see a real geometry.

Becoming a consultancy

Service work funds us and teaches us. It also absorbs every hour. If nothing is productised by the third engagement, we have accidentally started an engineering firm.

What it becomes

The metric transfers

Validated designs per GPU-hour is domain-agnostic. Whatever we learn about screening cheaply, surrogating aggressively and failing fast applies to any problem with a slow oracle.

The compute layer

Running solvers and models together efficiently is a real product: scheduling, caching, surrogate reuse, cost-aware search. Strong second act, weak first one, because it has no domain wedge and competes with well-funded infrastructure companies. Earn it by proving the loop somewhere specific first.

Lab in the loop

The architecture does not care whether the oracle is a solver or an experiment. Swap the solver adapter for a robotic rig and the constraint library for a protocol library, and this becomes a self-driving lab. Far larger prize, far slower loop. The right order is to prove the machinery where evaluations cost cents, then move to where they cost days.

Research, not just engineering

Engineering design is the proof of concept because the oracle is trustworthy and the answer is checkable. The same loop points at any field with a validated simulator and an unexplored design space.

Owning the scoreboard

Several of these fields have no public benchmark. Whoever publishes the standard comparison set defines what better means, and everyone else has to answer it.

The surrogate library compounds

Every customer run trains a model of a geometry family. Those models are useless to a competitor and increasingly valuable to us, which is the only durable data advantage available here.

10

When we stop

Kill criterion

Take a design the customer already built and is proud of. Beat it by 15% or more on their primary metric, in their solver, inside their manufacturing constraints, within two weeks of wall-clock time.

If we cannot, the solver was not good enough or the search space was not the bottleneck. Move to the next candidate. Do not spend another month tuning the agent.

11

Open questions for the team

Bring an answer, not an opinion.

Q1

Do we pick the boring problem that funds us, or the hard one we want to work on? They are not the same row in the matrix.

Q2

Are the weights right? Re-score the top ten independently and bring your numbers.

Q3

Which solver licences do we need and what do they cost per core-hour? This may decide the problem for us.

Q4

Who is the first customer by name? Not a segment. A company and a person we can email this week.

Q5

What do we do if a customer agrees the design is better and still will not build it? That failure is more likely than losing on performance.

Q6

What is the smallest experiment that tells us whether the agent layer beats a plain optimiser? If we cannot design that test, we do not have a thesis.

Q7

What autonomy level do we actually sell at? L1 is honest and slow, L3 is where the economics work. Guessing wrong sets the wrong price and the wrong expectation.
