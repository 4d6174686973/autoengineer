The idea as a business

One technology, four possible companies. They differ in who pays, how fast we learn, and how much capital we need.

The technical bet is written up elsewhere. This page ignores whether it works and asks the other question: if it works, what do we sell, to whom, and does that add up to a company. Four shapes, one canvas each.

The commercial bet

We are not selling optimisation. We are selling engineering hours we removed. A design study costs a good engineer weeks. If we cut that to days and the part is measurably better, the value is easy to price and easy to check.

The risk is not demand, it is proof. Every engineering team has hard design problems. Almost none will buy software they cannot verify. So the business model has to produce evidence before it asks for money. That points to services first, product later — not the other way round.

01

Four shapes

Same core loop underneath. What changes is where we sit relative to the customer's engineering team.

Shape A

Design service

We take the customer's problem and deliver a design. They pay per project. We keep the tooling.

Sell: a finished design package
Capital: low
Learning: fastest
Shape B

Software

The loop runs inside their environment, on their solver. Licence per seat or per compute.

Sell: a tool
Capital: medium
Learning: slow, gated by procurement
Shape C

Own products

We design a part, have it manufactured, sell it. Cold plates, magnets, heat exchangers.

Sell: hardware
Capital: high
Learning: slow, but margin is ours
Shape D

Vertical stack

One problem class, done properly. Our own surrogate, our own solver settings, sold as a narrow tool or as a subscription to a design portal.

Sell: a solved category
Capital: medium
Learning: deep but narrow

These are not exclusive and not permanent. A is how you learn. D is how you get a defensible position. B is how you scale it. C is how you capture the most value per design, if we ever get manufacturing right.

02

Canvas A — Design service

We are an engineering firm with unusual tooling. The customer sees results, not software.

Key partners

  • Simulation vendors (Ansys, Siemens, COMSOL) for licences
  • Cloud compute provider
  • Contract manufacturers for prototypes
  • University groups for validation

Key activities

  • Run customer design studies
  • Build reusable problem templates
  • Validate against their baseline part

Key resources

  • The agent loop
  • Two or three engineers who can talk to customers
  • Compute budget

Value proposition

  • A better part than the one they have, or we do not charge full price
  • Weeks of engineering time returned
  • They keep the IP and the sign-off
  • No procurement of new software

Customer relationships

  • Direct, project by project
  • Named engineer on their side
  • Repeat work if the first study lands

Channels

  • Personal network
  • Conferences and technical talks
  • Published benchmark results

Customer segments

  • Mid-size firms with hard thermal or electromagnetic problems and no optimisation team
  • R&D groups in large firms with a stuck design
  • Hardware startups without simulation staff

Cost structure

  • Salaries, dominant
  • Compute and solver licences per project
  • Sales cycle is cheap but slow

Revenue streams

  • Fixed-price study, roughly €20k–€80k
  • Success fee tied to an agreed metric
  • Retainer once they come back a third time

Why start here

It is the only shape where the first euro arrives before the product exists. Every project is a labelled dataset, a reference customer and a template we keep. It also forces us to find out which problems are actually hard for people who pay.

Why it is not the company

Revenue scales with headcount. Margins are consultancy margins. Investors price it accordingly. It only becomes a company if the templates compound into something we can sell without us in the room.

03

Canvas B — Software

The loop ships as a product. It runs on their solver, on their machines or ours.

Key partners

  • Simulation vendors — partner or competitor, decided early
  • Cloud and on-prem HPC providers
  • Model providers, until we run our own
  • System integrators inside large firms

Key activities

  • Solver adapters
  • Reliability of unattended runs
  • Security review, on-prem deployment
  • Support

Key resources

  • Adapter library across solvers
  • Benchmarks we can show
  • Engineering team that can support enterprise customers

Value proposition

  • Their existing simulation setup, run thousands of times instead of dozens
  • No data leaves their network
  • Their engineers stay in control at the gates that matter
  • Cheaper than hiring an optimisation team

Customer relationships

  • Pilot, then annual contract
  • Heavy onboarding for the first problem class
  • Long relationships, slow to start

Channels

  • Direct enterprise sales
  • Resale through simulation vendors
  • Open benchmarks and papers

Customer segments

  • Automotive, aerospace, energy, semiconductor firms with in-house simulation
  • Teams already running parameter studies by hand
  • Not: firms without a validated solver

Cost structure

  • R&D, dominant
  • Inference and compute
  • Enterprise sales, expensive and long

Revenue streams

  • Annual licence, €50k–€500k depending on seats and deployment
  • Usage-based compute markup
  • Paid pilots

What has to be true

Runs must survive without us watching them. A customer engineer must be able to set up a new problem in a day. And we need results on their solver, not ours, that they can reproduce.

The main threat

Ansys or Siemens ship the same feature. They own the solver, the customer relationship and the data. Our answer has to be that we work across solvers and they never will, plus a head start on the parts that are hard to copy.

04

Canvas C — Own products

We use the tool on ourselves, manufacture, and sell parts. Nobody has to trust our software.

Key partners

  • Contract manufacturers, additive and conventional
  • Material suppliers
  • Test labs and certification bodies
  • Distributors for the first market

Key activities

  • Design one part family very well
  • Qualification and testing
  • Supply chain and quality
  • Selling hardware

Key resources

  • The designs themselves, protected or kept quiet
  • Manufacturing relationships
  • Test data proving performance

Value proposition

  • A part that outperforms what is on the market on a number the buyer already tracks
  • Example: cold plates with lower thermal resistance at the same pressure drop
  • Example: magnets with reduced heavy rare earth content
  • Buyer needs no opinion about how it was designed

Customer relationships

  • Qualification project, then supply agreement
  • Switching costs are high once designed in

Channels

  • Direct to OEM engineering teams
  • Distributors
  • Trade fairs

Customer segments

  • Datacentre and power electronics builders (cooling)
  • Motor and generator manufacturers (magnets)
  • Anyone whose product is limited by one physical quantity

Cost structure

  • Tooling, prototypes, inventory
  • Qualification, slow and expensive
  • Cost of goods, permanent

Revenue streams

  • Unit sales at hardware margins
  • NRE for customer-specific designs
  • Licensing a design to an incumbent manufacturer

The appeal

We capture the full value of a better design instead of a licence fee. No procurement conversation about AI. The product either performs or it does not.

The problem

This is a hardware company. Capital, qualification cycles, inventory, quality systems. It also puts us in one market, so a wrong first pick is expensive. Do not start here.

05

Canvas D — Vertical stack

Pick one problem class. Own it end to end: surrogate, solver settings, manufacturing rules, benchmark.

Key partners

  • Two or three design partners in the chosen class
  • One manufacturer who tells us what is buildable
  • Compute provider

Key activities

  • Train a surrogate on our own solver runs
  • Encode manufacturing rules for that class
  • Publish benchmarks against the standard design

Key resources

  • Simulation data for one geometry family — hard to copy
  • The surrogate, which makes us fast and cheap per design
  • Reputation in one narrow field

Value proposition

  • Upload requirements, get a validated design back in hours
  • Cheap per design because the surrogate replaces most solver runs
  • Manufacturable by construction
  • We know this class better than a generalist tool ever will

Customer relationships

  • Self-serve after the first guided design
  • Low touch, priced per design

Channels

  • Web portal
  • Word of mouth inside one industry
  • Published comparisons

Customer segments

  • Everyone designing that one type of part
  • Including firms too small to run simulation at all

Cost structure

  • Up-front simulation to build the surrogate, large and one-off
  • Marginal cost per design near zero afterwards
  • Narrow market means narrow ceiling

Revenue streams

  • Per design, €2k–€20k
  • Subscription for teams designing continuously
  • Combines with C: sell the design or sell the part

This is the shape with the clearest moat. The asset is simulation data nobody else has for a geometry family we chose. It is also the shape that most depends on picking the right class, which is what the candidate matrix in the technical brief is for.

07

A sequence that works

Each stage pays for the next and produces the evidence the next one needs.

Months 0–6

Two paid studies

Two customers, one problem class if possible. Fixed price. We do the work, they get the design. Goal is not margin, it is a reproducible before/after number.

Proof neededA part measurably better than their baseline, verified on their solver.
Months 6–15

Pick the class, build the surrogate

Take the class where the studies went best. Spend real compute generating data. Encode the manufacturing rules. Publish a benchmark.

Proof neededDesign turnaround drops from weeks to hours at equal quality.
Months 15–30

Sell designs, not hours

Priced per design. Same team, more customers. This is where revenue stops tracking headcount.

Proof neededA customer buys a second design without us in the room.
Then

Widen or deepen

Widen into software across solvers and classes, or deepen into manufacturing our own parts. The evidence from stage three decides which.

Proof neededSecond class is faster to stand up than the first.

09

What would change the answer

Arguments against

Customers cannot state the objective

If getting a clean problem spec takes weeks per customer, every shape becomes a service business. Watch this in the first two studies.

The baseline is already good

Decades of hand-tuning leave little on the table in mature parts. A 3% improvement does not sell. Pick classes where nobody has optimised properly.

Simulation licences

Running thousands of solves may cost more in licence fees than the design is worth. Open-source solvers or a vendor deal are the only ways round this. Check before committing to a class.

Arguments for

The bottleneck is real and visible

Engineers describe the same problem unprompted: too few evaluations, too much setup. We do not have to create the need.

Evidence is cheap to produce

Unlike most AI claims, ours is checkable. Their solver, their metric, their part. That shortens sales conversations.

The data asset compounds

Every study makes the next one cheaper. That is the only reason this becomes something other than a consultancy.

10

When to stop

If after four paid studies we have not produced one design a customer preferred to their own, on their solver, we do not have a business — we have a research project.

Then either narrow to a single class where we can control everything, or stop. Do not raise money to keep looking.

11

Open questions

Q1

Do we take the first paying customer we can get, or do we wait for one in the class we want to own?

Q2

Partner with a simulation vendor or stay independent? This decides shape B and it is hard to reverse.

Q3

Grant money for the surrogate compute, or equity? Grants are slower but the timeline may allow it.

Q4

Are we willing to run a hardware company? If nobody wants to, drop shape C now and stop mentioning it.
